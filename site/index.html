02 · Problem

In orbit, a missed catch doesn't fall

A 5 × 5 × 15 cm, 0.5 kg bar drifts past a 7-DoF arm at 2–15 cm/s while tumbling at up to 30°/s. Nothing pulls it back: a bounced object just leaves. So the goal isn't contact, it's a soft capture that ends with the object held and still, using only a 30 Hz pose sensor with 5 mm and 2° of noise and 5% dropped readings.

03 · Method

Five stages, one moving frame

  1. 01

    Estimate

    Kalman filter for position and velocity; sliding-window fit for spin. Velocity within 1 cm/s after a median 0.70 s.

  2. 02

    Plan the intercept

    Sweep capture times along the predicted path. Take the earliest grasp, square to a face, that the arm reaches in time and holds through the stop clear of singularities.

  3. 03

    Ride along

    Work in the bar's moving, spinning frame: line up behind it, ride 10 cm back, then slide in matching drift and spin.

  4. 04

    Grasp only if safe

    Close only when the estimated miss is under 1 cm and 2 cm/s; otherwise back off and re-plan. Fingers close fast, then slowly through contact.

  5. 05

    Stop gently

    Bring drift and spin to rest together on a smooth profile, so the grip only supplies a small, steady force.

05 · What testing changed

Found in simulation, fixed in code

Spin 20 → 44°/s

The hand chased itself

Once the fingers touched, the estimator saw the hand moving the bar and the hand followed the estimate. Freezing the target motion when closing starts fixed it.

Kick 5.6 → 3.8 cm/s

Close in two speeds

Fast to 5 mm short of the bar, then slowly through contact. The worst-case kick halved, from 10.6 to 5.3 cm/s.

Caught 65 → 85%

Plan again, try every grasp

The planner committed to a grasp from above and gave up after one try. Trying each face and re-planning after a miss lifted the catch rate without touching the grasp.

06 · Limitations

What the robot can't do (yet)

The 85% holds inside the envelope it was tested in. Outside it, or on real hardware, these are the walls it hits first, and what would move each one.

  • One workspace. The base is fixed, so the catch has to happen within 0.82 m of the shoulder and 15 cm above the deck. A path that never crosses that zone can't be caught: 8% of throws. A mobile or free-flying base would widen it.
  • Slow objects only. Tested up to 15 cm/s drift and 30°/s spin. At 11–15 cm/s the catch rate drops to 76%: the arm needs up to 2.5 s to get into position at the Panda's joint-speed limits, and fast throws are gone by then.
  • One object, one grip. A known 5×5×15 cm, 0.5 kg rigid bar. The gripper opens 8 cm, so it can only close across the bar's 5 cm faces. Larger, irregular, flexible or unknown objects need a different hand and grasp planner.
  • Gentle stops, no force sensing. Braking is capped at 0.3 m/s² and 1 rad/s² under position control, and grip force isn't measured, so a slip goes unnoticed. Heavier or faster objects need a wrist force/torque sensor and admittance control.
  • Joint limits. The planner keeps clear of the stretched-elbow and wrist singularities, but the approach clips at joint limits instead of planning around them, behind some of the 5% aborted at the grasp gate.
  • Idealized sensing. A 30 Hz pose sensor (5 mm / 2° noise, 5% dropout) that always knows which object it sees. No cameras, occlusion, glare or model fitting: vision is the biggest step to hardware.
  • Planning time. About 0.5 s of pure-Python IK while the simulation waits (about 0.1 s in the browser). Compiled IK or a precomputed reachability map would make it real-time.
  • Simulation only. The base is bolted to an inertial world, so the arm's motion never pushes it back as it would on a free-floating spacecraft. No actuator delay, joint flexibility or contact uncertainty beyond MuJoCo's model. Never run on hardware.
